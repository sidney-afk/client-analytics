From 2026-06-20 through the 2026-09-20 cutoff, SyncView first reproduced Linear's production workflow—projects, batches, deliverables, assignments, statuses, comments, assets and intake—then made the ordinary production path native and stopped outbound mirroring and its parity exception. The repository absorbed 827 identifiable merged PRs (283 squash commits and 544 PR merge commits), within 2,886 reachable commits; 727 non-merge commits touched `index.html` and 432 changed only `docs/`. These are repository-wide counts, not a claim that every change belonged to the exit. The result was a working replacement with unfinished retirement work, not the disappearance of every Linear dependency. The next project should preserve that behavior while splitting the roughly 90,000-line file; it should not inherit this project's migration machinery or quietly finish its backlog.

## What actually caught bugs

Ranking below uses conservative, explicitly attributable findings in the requested reading sample, not an invented project-wide defect total. Findings overlap between practices and must not be summed. Tests written after discovery are regression protection, not credited with the original discovery. References to repairs mean [OPEN_REPAIRS](../ops/OPEN_REPAIRS.md); dated journal entries and D-rulings mean the [exit journal](../ops/LINEAR_EXIT_JOURNAL.md).

1. **Codex review: at least 14 concrete findings in five repair records.** Repair 204/204a records four: the deploy-trigger claim was wrong, a Drive 404 did not establish permanent disappearance, the new aggregate bucket broke older callers, and the replacement marker followed the wrong file. Repair 211 records four successive counterexamples to proposed escaping fixes in [PR #1406](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1406). Repair 212 credits three corrections in [#1422](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1422): preserving approval stamps, rechecking concurrent changes and correcting the operator command. Repair 220 credits two parent-resolution findings in [#1444](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1444). Repair 221 credits the rejection of an unnecessary native-parent fallback in [#1447](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1447). This was useful review because it contradicted the implementation with specific cases. It also exposed how often the author had supplied tests that agreed with the author's mistake.

2. **Measurement before prescription: at least five distinct failure classes.** Repairs 205, 206, 207, 208 and 210 respectively exposed chronically red monitoring treated as healthy, identifying data printed by probes, serialized network work, excessive client-side recomputation, and inconsistent assignee identity resolution. Live read-only measurement was valuable; so was an offline fixture with live-scale shape. The particularly expensive correction is 207 followed by 208: making the network faster did not fix the owner's six-second experience. A zero-latency fixture still took 8.5 seconds to display and 105.5 seconds to settle. Profiling, not another network explanation, located the dominant work. Do not import live access into a file split merely because measurement worked here: measure the built application and local fixtures first.

3. **Owner drills and representative journeys: at least four escaped defects.** Repair 214/216 caught the editor picker counting a 3,232-row population through a 1,000-row response; repair 215 caught selecting text and releasing outside a dialog discarding the draft; repair 220 caught native parents missing from Create Post. The journal's 2026-09-18 native TEST-attribution repair, [#1414](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1414), caught the real browser's TEST identity being rejected where fixtures supplied an ordinary client identity. These were small, realistic journeys that challenged the fixtures. The 2026-09-14 custody drill established download-and-restore, but its “second device” claim was later withdrawn: successful execution did not license a larger claim.

4. **Independent supervisor/executor verification: at least three corrections to asserted coverage.** The 2026-09-16 “guard-count set is NINE” entry corrected the claimed two/three-site scope and found a name-set comparison that changing a number could not fix. D35 records the 2026-09-17 repair still omitting `authenticated` from sequence revocation. The 2026-09-15 “supervisor's claim was the thing that failed” entry found both reviewers were reasoning over shallow history. Keep the independent read, not the hierarchy: the supervisor was also wrong, and verification in the other direction mattered.

5. **Offline execution, including source-slicing tests: at least two regression incidents.** Repair 208 says moving caches outside extracted functions broke five suites; those failures exposed a real dependency of the current harness. Repair 220 says the Production structure browser subtest caught incorrect adoption of a Linear-born root after the parent fix. These are two incidents, not six bugs. Credit source slicing for cheap execution of shipped functions, not for proving the whole application: D28 records an extracted `applySettledWorld` using an out-of-scope `j`, while export/call/single-source assertions passed without executing it. Moving code and finding its name are different proofs.

6. **Identity-exposure gate: one explicitly documented catch, with a material blind spot.** [PR #1371](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1371) went red for public identity exposure; `CLAUDE.md` explicitly records the missed pre-push check. Keep the committed-diff gate. Repair 206 records that the same repository protection did not stop probes printing 43 client slugs into execution logs. “The gate passed” never meant “every output is safe.” Public summaries need aggregate counts, not roster examples.

**Useful controls with no independent bug-discovery count established here:** runtime flags and rollback provided containment and a reversible transition; they were not detectors. The cutoff close-out records outbound disabled before parity, the reverse of the plan, with the owner accepting the approximately nine-minute interval. That is evidence of an operated switch, not evidence that a rollback caught a bug. Repair 217 also found browser legacy exits outside the outbound switch's coverage. The TEST-client-only rule bounded mutation damage, but the attribution failure above shows that a TEST result cannot automatically stand for ordinary production behavior. Retain bounded synthetic testing; do not confuse safety scope with representative coverage.

**The journal and OPEN_REPAIRS were memory, not independent detectors.** Their strongest contribution is preserving failed claims, reproduction details and why a fix changed. Repair 204a's failed sabotage control, repair 208's five broken suites and D28's unexecuted function are immediately useful to the next project. No separate discovery is credited merely because somebody wrote it down. The ledger did not even contain the picker report when a handover said it did (journal, 2026-09-19 “editor-picker regression was not in the ledger”). Keep a concise failure record with evidence; its existence is not assurance.

## What cost time without catching anything

“No catch established” below describes the observed overhead, not a proof that the practice was useless everywhere.

- **The four-document Production ritual had no demonstrated independent catch in this sample.** `AGENTS.md` requires keeping `WIRED-PARITY.md`, the Production foundation audit, `EXECUTION_LOG.md` and `ROLLBACK.md` current. The documents serve different purposes, but repeating a status across all four is not four checks. The counterexample is repair 204a: review, not the written deploy story, caught that deployment was automatic. Repair 210 found the deploy manifest still described July source. For a no-behavior-change split, one reviewed change record and a tested artifact rollback are enough; update an existing contract when it actually changes.

- **Number allocation and parallel journal edits created reconciliation work.** Repair 202 preserves six duplicated ledger numbers rather than pretending they never collided. Repair 216 and the journal's two 2026-09-19 picker corrections document two sessions claiming 214, followed by renumbering and a corrective note about the corrective note. The 2026-09-19 Step 29c record also describes a draft overwriting the journal title. None of that found a product bug. Stable entry IDs and one owner of the current checkpoint would remove the collision without removing history.

- **Gates discovered during the actual dispatch consumed the expensive window.** D34 says the deploy preflight had been written, reviewed and pinned for days before its first live execution refused 10 of 156 keys inside Step 19. The refusal was useful; postponing the read was not. The 2026-09-17 notify-deploy record adds a different failure: a BOM caused source attestation to fail after the functions had already deployed. A file split needs build, boot and rollback checks exercised before publication, not a new collection of untried release gates.

- **Prose was repeatedly mistaken for current state.** The [cutoff runbook](../ops/LINEAR_CUTOFF_RUNBOOK.md) still opens as prepared/not executed with a September 15 premise; the September 20 close-out records the actual cutoff. The [master sequence](../independence/LINEAR_EXIT_MASTER_SEQUENCE.md) records a coordinator inspecting main but missing a second exit program in open PRs. It also records tracing a dead create path and treating a code-default flag as live state. The [Step 29b inventory](../ops/LINEAR_EXIT_STEP29B_INVENTORY.md) corrected the workflow count to 50. Maintaining more copies of the same assertion did not make it true.

- **Confident explanations substituted for inexpensive checks.** The sampled September 16 entries retract the claim that a restored database necessarily changes the checked system identifier, and the claim that PostgreSQL 17 was unavailable when its package repository was merely unconfigured. The September 15 fabricated-hash entry is worse: a printed prefix was completed with invented characters before being corrected. Repair 216 describes dismissing failures as pre-existing against a local main that was 20 commits stale. These were authoring failures, not difficult migration problems.

- **Receipt/gate collisions and bad test environments generated repeat work.** The September 16 dated-receipt/live-gate entry records three failures in one day on the same manifest: changing its pins falsified history, while preserving them blocked current work. D22's September 17 run reports were repeatedly corrected as port allocation, fixture-root, Node-version and shallow-history problems were separated from actual failures. D29 is the resulting rule: matching a broken baseline establishes absence of a new regression, not validity. Neither repinning nor rerunning an invalid setup makes evidence stronger.

- **Repeated documentation review was partly preventable, not categorically wasted.** The September 19 intake entries repeatedly repair flag coverage, deployment ordering, circular acceptance and stale-browser closure claims around [#1432](https://github.com/sidney-afk/client-analytics/pull/1432). Those are real operational errors, so abandoning review would be the wrong lesson. The avoidable cost was drafting executable instructions before checking the reachable route and deployment boundary. Review #1418 also corrected “all seven” blockers closed when only four were supported; #1420 corrected incomplete evidence for a three-part check. More confident closure prose was creating work for the next reviewer.

## The five rules to carry into a pure refactor and the five to drop

**Carry:**

1. Execute each moved path and compare externally visible results, including boot order, global scope, events, requests and persisted state; declarations alone missed the D28 `j` failure.
2. Pin one current baseline and reproduce failures there before classifying them; repair 216 and the shallow-history correction disprove “pre-existing” by assertion.
3. Use independent review with concrete counterexamples and verify the reviewer too; the 14 Codex findings and the supervisor's failed claim justify both halves.
4. Keep representative offline journeys and bounded synthetic data, including native-only rows and realistic population sizes; the TEST-attribution and capped-picker drills show what small convenient fixtures missed.
5. Keep the public-identity check and one concise evidence/rollback record for each structural slice; #1371, repair 206 and the receipt/gate collision define both their value and their limits.

**Drop:**

1. Do not require the four-document Production update ritual for every file move; its unproven incremental detection and the stale deploy story argue for one current checkpoint.
2. Do not carry authority flips, new runtime flags, database custody or live mutation drills into a behavior-preserving split; the cutoff controls addressed a different class of change.
3. Do not renumber a shared ledger or duplicate current status across parallel journals; repairs 202/216 show avoidable collisions, so use stable IDs and one checkpoint owner.
4. Do not use dated receipts as mutable current-source gates or fix drift by hand-repinning; the September 16 manifest deadlock and fabricated hash show why.
5. Do not accept passing string-presence checks, an old red aggregate gate or a broken baseline as behavior proof; D28/D29 and repair 220 require execution and failure-level comparison.
